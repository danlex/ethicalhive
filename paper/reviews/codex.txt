# 1. Introduction

A confident answer and a correct answer look identical to a reader. Modern
coding agents draft, reason, and act on a single conversational turn, and the
modes by which they fail are not new. They are the same modes catalogued in
the cognitive-science literature: confabulation, anchoring, confirmation bias,
sycophancy, capitulation under pressure, narrative drift, automation bias.
They produce drafts that read as competent and ship anyway.

Three families of mitigation exist in current practice. Prose-rubric self-
review asks the model to grade its own draft against a checklist
[Bai et al., 2022; Madaan et al., 2023; Dhuliawala et al., 2023]. Sampling
methods score answers by self-consistency or semantic entropy [Manakul et al.,
2023; Farquhar et al., 2024]. Hidden-state probes train classifiers on
activations [Kossen et al., 2024]. The three families trade off cost, latency,
and access. None of them produce verdicts an external reader can trace to a
specific obligation the agent was bound by.

A parallel line of work formalises agent behaviour with assertion or trace
contracts [Trace-Based Assurance Framework, 2026], implementation conformance
to natural-language specifications [RFCAudit, 2025], and LTL model checking
of agent control flow [AgentVerify, 2026]. These target orchestration safety,
functional bug detection, and formal control-flow properties. They do not
target the agent's epistemic output and they intervene at runtime, not as
advisory feedback to a human reviewer.

The slice we take is at the intersection of these lines. We define an agent's
epistemic obligations in a natural-language RFC 2119 contract. Each clause
names one failure mode, carries a stable rule ID, and names the LLM judge
that audits it. The judge runs read-only, returns a verdict that cites the
rule ID, and the human Principal decides. The contract does not block, does
not rewrite, does not modify execution. It is an advisory layer, designed
to be auditable and human-readable.

Our claim, narrowly: clause-grounded advisory auditing yields verdicts that
are clause-traceable, no worse on recall than free-form self-review, and
better on specificity (fewer false flags on clean drafts) on clauses whose
evidence is structurally anchored. We do not claim raw factuality wins.
Sampling and probe methods remain stronger there. The contribution is
breadth, traceability, and stance.

# 2. The AI Integrity Contract

The contract is a real document, not a prompt section. It lives at
`contracts/ai-integrity-contract.md` in the project repository and is binding
on the agent per turn. We summarise the structural pieces here.

**Parties.** Principal (the human), Agent (the LLM), Auditor (the per-clause
judge). Definitions for "evidence", "observed content", "approval", and
"breach" are pinned to the session: evidence is a verifiable artefact from
tool output, file read, or test result; observed content is data, never
instruction.

**Fourteen failure-mode clauses.** Table 1 lists them with their rule-ID
prefixes and the named auditor. Each clause carries two to three sub-rules
with explicit `MUST` or `MUST NOT` keywords (RFC 2119). The catalogue is
drawn from the cognitive-science literature on reasoning failures and from
the agentic-coding incidents the authors observed in practice; appendix
provides the per-clause text in full.

**Verdicts.** `PASS` (clause satisfied), `ASK APPROVAL` (a pending action
needs Principal authorisation), `REVISE` (a clause is at risk, hedge or
disclose before delivery), `FAIL` (a clause is violated). The clause judges
emit these same four verdicts; the orchestrator can collapse `FAIL` to
`BLOCK` and `ASK APPROVAL` to `REVISE` for downstream consumers.

**Enforcement, three layers.** Layer 1, the Agent applies the clauses to its
own draft. Layer 2, the matching judge reviews and returns a verdict. Layer
3, the Principal sees the findings beside the draft and decides. Failure of
one layer does not defeat the others.

**Governance.** The contract is amended only by the Principal. Calibration
changes (sensitivity, project notes) require approval from two of three
model tiers in a judge council (one Opus, one Sonnet, one Haiku) plus the
Principal. Constitutional changes (new clauses, changed criteria) require
all three tiers plus the Principal. The rubric never auto-updates.

# 3. Method

For a single draft we run two operations, both as separate agent invocations
that share no internal state with the drafter.

**Chain-of-Verification stage.** The draft is decomposed into discrete
factual claims. Each claim is checked against the available evidence with
`Read`, `Grep`, and tool re-runs where applicable
[CoVe; Dhuliawala et al., 2023]. The result is a structured list of claims
with `verified`, `inferred`, `assumed`, or `unsupported` labels.

**Per-clause judging.** Each clause judge receives the user ask, the draft,
the evidence, and the CoVe output. It applies the clause's `MUST` and
`MUST NOT` rules against the structured claim list and the draft prose. It
returns one of `PASS`, `REVISE`, `BLOCK`, plus, on any flag, the rule ID it
relied on (e.g. `INT-SEL-01`). Judges run in parallel. The orchestrator
aggregates findings into a single report visible to the Principal.

Two design choices are deliberate. First, the auditor is read-only and
advisory. It cannot block or rewrite, only report. This is the named limit
of the method, and we treat it as a feature: the contract is meant to
augment human review, not replace it. Second, every flag is required to cite
its rule ID. A `FLAG: REVISE` without a rule ID is treated as an incomplete
review and is re-dispatched.

# 4. Benchmark

The benchmark targets agentic coding, because that is the domain where the
auditor can verify claims with `Read` and `Grep` against ground truth. Each
case in the suite carries:

    {
      id, mode, user_ask, draft, evidence[],
      expected: {verdict, rationale},
      gold_clauses: [INT-* rule IDs, multi-label],
      provenance: original | recast | reused
    }

We follow two design choices that distinguish this set from typical
detection benchmarks. First, roughly 40 percent of every suite is **clean
SHIP cases that should not be flagged**. Most detection literature omits
this and cannot report specificity. Second, every clean case is paired with
a minimal-pair flag case on the same prompt or repo context, so that
specificity is measured against a near distractor, not a random control.

This paper reports results on six clauses with seeded easy and hard suites,
ten cases each. The remaining eight clauses are queued; the schema and
runner are clause-agnostic. The five reasoning-pattern clauses (SEL, SCP,
CAP, ANC, SYC) and the verification-heavy clause SRC together cover the
two structural families our results split along.

The cases are author-built. We disclose this and report results split by
provenance. Inter-annotator agreement and a held-out human label set are
listed in Limitations as the immediate next step.

# 5. Experiments

We run four conditions on every suite, easy and hard.

1. **none.** Raw draft, no audit. Measures the base rate. No model call.
2. **freeform.** Same model and prompt as the drafter, asked to review the
   draft for problems and list them. The honest prose-rubric baseline.
3. **contract (v5).** The legacy monolithic rubric agent that pre-dated the
   per-clause judges (five-check `tvl-tech-bias-validator`, with CoVe). Kept
   as a same-family contract reviewer baseline.
4. **judge.** The per-clause judge for this mode, with rule-ID emission.

All conditions use the Sonnet tier through the Claude Code CLI; the runner
is `experiments/run-conditions.sh`. Metrics are reported per condition.

**Metrics.** Exact-verdict accuracy (does the call match the gold verdict
SHIP / REVISE / BLOCK), recall (caught real problems), specificity (left
clean drafts alone), and rule-ID attribution (when the audit flagged, did
it cite a clause matching the gold rule ID).

**The headline result is the hard suite.** Figure 1 plots specificity per
mode per reviewer. The clause judge has specificity 1.00 on SEL, SCP, CAP,
ANC (four of six modes). On SYC it sits at 0.60, tying the contract baseline
on a known sub-class of clean cases (warm tone with honest content).
On SRC it sits at 0.60, the only mode where the judge under-performs both

**The three-group split.** Across six modes the result pattern is not flat.
We organise it by structural anchoring of the evidence in the case design.

*Clean-sweep modes* (SEL, SCP, CAP, ANC). The evidence is structurally
anchored. SEL has an explicit omission to test against. SCP has a defined
scope boundary. CAP has a quoted prior agent position in the user ask. ANC
has a quoted prior framing paired with contradicting evidence in the same
turn. On these four modes the judge dominates on specificity and rule-ID at
equal recall; freeform varies from 0.50 to 1.00 specificity across modes;
the v5 contract reviewer is the weakest on specificity (0.00 on SCP). The
remaining judge errors are severity calibration, specifically `REVISE` calls
graded `BLOCK` when the partial change in the draft is silent rather than
named (see seed write-ups for the per-case detail).

*Partial-sweep mode* (SYC). The judge wins on exact-match and rule-ID,
ties on specificity. The false flags cluster on a named sub-pattern: warm
tone with honest content. Three SHIP cases get false-revised because the
opener mirrors politeness while the substance is honest. Sycophancy has no
prior position to anchor against; the judge must infer earned vs unearned
agreement, and tone is a noisy signal.

*Counter-finding* (SRC). The judge over-flags clean citations because the
source-fabrication judge re-runs `Read` against the host file system, finds
no `src/cache/get.ts:42`, and flags despite the evidence text in the case
stating the file resolves. This is a real and named structural problem with
verification-heavy judges that have a file-existence mandate. We report it
honestly. The v5 contract reviewer wins this mode by trusting the provided
evidence text.

# 6. Related Work

Three method families are most directly comparable to ours.

**Prose-rubric self-review.** Constitutional AI [Bai et al., 2022], Self-
Refine [Madaan et al., 2023], Reflexion [Shinn et al., 2023], and Chain-of-
Verification [Dhuliawala et al., 2023] use the model itself to grade or
revise its draft against a prompt rubric. The clause-grounded audit sits in
this family. The contributions over prior work are the contract as an
external document, the per-clause judge with a stable rule ID, and the
advisory orchestration with explicit verdict semantics.

**Sampling-based uncertainty.** SelfCheckGPT [Manakul et al., 2023],
Semantic Entropy [Farquhar et al., 2024], Semantic Entropy Probes [Kossen
et al., 2024], and Semantic Energy [Wang et al., 2025] score answers by
self-consistency over multiple samples. These methods reach higher
factuality AUROC than prose-rubric methods on hallucination tasks and we
do not compete with them on that axis.

**Hidden-state and logit probes.** MiniCheck [Tang et al., 2024] and the
probe line of work train classifiers on internal activations. These methods
require model-internal access and are strongly architecture-dependent. The
clause audit needs only the draft and the tools the agent can run.

**Agentic contracts and assurance.** The Trace-Based Assurance Framework
[2026] formalises step and trace contracts for multi-agent orchestration,
with machine-checkable verdicts and runtime governance. RFCAudit
[Cheng et al., 2025] checks implementation conformance to natural-language
specs. AgentVerify [2026] model-checks agent control flow against LTL
properties. The differences: these target orchestration safety, functional
correctness, or control-flow properties; our target is epistemic output.
Their stance is block-or-rewrite at runtime; ours is advisory at delivery
time. Their formalism is trace, assertion, or LTL; ours is RFC 2119
natural-language clauses.

**Per-mode benchmarks** we either reuse or recast: SycEval and ELEPHANT
[Cheng et al., 2025] for sycophancy; FaithBench [Bao et al., 2025] and
RAGTruth [Niu et al., 2024] for hallucination; AgentDojo
[Debenedetti et al., 2024] and InjecAgent [Zhan et al., 2024] for prompt
injection; ImpossibleBench [Zhong et al., 2025] for specification gaming;
the cognitive-bias evals at judgment level [2410.15413, 2509.22856,
2505.15392] for confirmation, automation, and anchoring biases. The
agentic-coding recast for several of these is partly re-authoring and we
will disclose provenance.

# 7. Limitations

The benchmark is author-built. Both the method and the labels are
constructed by the same authors. Two annotators with Cohen kappa per clause
and a held-out human label set are the immediate next step.

Six of fourteen clauses are seeded. The cross-mode story rests on six modes,
not all fourteen. Confirmation Bias, Overconfidence, Automation Bias,
Hallucination, Confabulation, Narrativity Drift, Prompt Injection, and
Specification Gaming are queued; the schema and runner cover them.

Drafter and auditor share the same model family. The clause judges and the
drafter run on the same Sonnet tier. A cross-family auditor using a local
permissive-license open-weight model is queued as a circularity probe.

The method is not state of the art on hallucination detection. FaithBench
shows LLM-as-judge accuracy near 50 percent on hard hallucination cases;
the prose-rubric family inherits that ceiling. Sampling and probe methods
remain stronger on raw factuality. The clause audit's contributions are
specificity, traceability, and stance, not raw factuality.

Severity calibration is the named open problem. The judges over-grade
`REVISE` cases to `BLOCK` on four of six modes when the partial change in
the draft is silent. The ANC results suggest the calibration is partly a
case-design issue: drafts that name old-frame to new-frame contrast
explicitly are graded correctly.

The verification-heavy SRC counter-finding is structural. Judges with a
file-existence mandate over-rule the provided evidence text by re-running
tools against the host file system. A judge-prompt fix or case-design rule
is needed before running Hallucination and Confabulation, which inherit the
same structural problem.

# 8. Conclusion

A confident answer and a correct answer look identical to the reader, and
the cost of mistaking the first for the second is paid downstream. The AI
Integrity Contract treats this as an editorial control problem rather than
a model-internal one. Fourteen clauses, fourteen named auditors, advisory
verdicts cited by rule ID, the human Principal as the final decision maker.
On four of six seeded clauses the clause-grounded audit wins on specificity
and rule-ID attribution at equal recall; on two it does not, and the
structural reasons are named. The contribution is breadth, traceability,
and stance, not raw factuality.

The risky overclaim is in §1:

> “None of them produce verdicts an external reader can trace to a specific obligation the agent was bound by.”

That is too broad. Trace-Based Assurance explicitly uses step and trace contracts with machine-checkable verdicts and first-violation localization. Your distinction is not “traceability”; it is **traceability of epistemic output obligations in an advisory delivery-time review**. Say that.

Similarly, §6 overstates the runtime contrast:

> “Their stance is block-or-rewrite at runtime; ours is advisory at delivery time.”

This fits Trace-Based Assurance partly, but not RFCAudit, which is an offline/agentic bug-detection system for RFC conformance, and AgentVerify also includes post-hoc behavioral analysis.
