EthicalAI: An Evidence-Linked Ontology of AI Integrity Failures

Alexandru Dan · 2026-11-09

Abstract

EthicalAI is an evidence-linked ontology of the ways an AI system can fail its integrity: fabricating a citation, keeping a user's disproved framing, exceeding a task without disclosure, and many more. Each behaviour is a record with a stable identifier, an operational definition, a worked example, typed relations to the obligations it violates, the harms it causes and the neighbouring behaviours it resembles, and a source passage behind each claim. The behaviours are organised into eleven categories, ten drawn from human cognitive systems and one for failures native to AI; the human-cognition parallels are marked as candidate behavioural tests, not claims of shared mechanism. Above the behaviours sit two principle layers: six top-level integrity principles that frame the catalogue, and seven operational obligations read off the records themselves, one most-central label per behaviour, across all 434, though an independent rater recovers that label only moderately (kappa about 0.4). Along that axis the two families separate: the failures borrowed from human cognition carry the honesty, calibration and independence obligations, while the AI-native tier carries the control-and-attack ones, corrigibility and non-maleficence, which have no human analogue. We describe the representation, the category scheme and the relation semantics, and report the construction and review of the historical 207-record study: 186 records grounded in worked examples from source papers, 21 retained without that support, a model-based codebook check of category recoverability with Cohen kappa 0.90 and 0.83, and a mapping onto twelve public incidents. A construction audit resolved citation and example errors against the sources before release. A later release extends the ontology to 434 records with 227 proposed behavioural analogies that stay outside the historical evaluation denominators. The counts are specific to this artifact; we claim no completeness and no validated set of distinct mechanisms.

1. Introduction

An AI assistant can cite a nonexistent case, keep a user's mistaken framing after finding contrary evidence, or extend a coding task without disclosing the extra changes. Each failure calls for a different check. Citation resolution does not test whether the assistant updated its framing, and correct framing does not establish that an action stayed within the agreed scope. A catalogue can make these distinctions available to a reviewer before they become a checklist, an experimental label or an audit obligation.

EthicalAI collects these behaviours in one ontology, organised around a charter of six integrity principles for AI: each record names a way an AI can fail one of them. The contribution is the combination rather than the size: an integrity-failure vocabulary in which each claim carries a source passage checked at construction, the entries are linked by a typed relation graph, and each is placed under the principle it violates and the harm it causes. A record carries a stable identifier, an operational definition, a worked example, evidence pointers, and typed relations to the obligation it violates, the harm it causes and the behaviours it resembles, so each claim stays traceable to what it rests on. Part of the vocabulary is drawn from human cognitive concepts; that parallel is a way to formulate a candidate behavioural test, not evidence that an AI system has the corresponding human mental process. Other records name attacks, system properties or outcomes, and their inclusion does not make them interchangeable scientific constructs.

This paper describes the ontology, its eleven-category scheme and its relation semantics, then reviews the historical 207-record study for grounding, category recoverability and incident coverage. A construction audit resolved citation and example errors against the sources before release.

A later release grew the ontology to 434 records by adding 227 proposed behavioural analogies. Section 5 keeps that expansion separate from the 207-record study, so the added entries and citations do not enlarge the evidence reported here.

2. Related resources

Existing resources provide overlapping views of AI failures. The MIT AI Risk Repository synthesises 74 frameworks containing 1,725 risks and offers causal and domain taxonomies [Slattery et al. 2024]. The NIST AI Risk Management Framework describes trustworthiness and governance practices [NIST 2023]. These resources organise risks and responsibilities at a broader level than an individual draft's evidence relationships. Their scope also includes causes and AI-originated failures, so they should not be reduced to lists of downstream harms.

Application-security resources overlap directly with this catalogue. The OWASP Top 10 includes prompt injection, excessive agency and misinformation [OWASP 2025]. Some of these failures involve an adversary and others need not. EthicalAI reuses these concerns within a vocabulary that also includes memory, reasoning, confidence and task-scope patterns. Its partial crosswalk records proposed correspondences; it does not establish equivalence between frameworks.

Behavioural benchmarks provide the closest comparison. Malberg et al. [2024] evaluate 30 cognitive biases across 20 models, with 30,000 tests. EthicalAI's classic-bias subset overlaps that work. Hallucination surveys and benchmarks, including Ji et al. [2022] and HALoGEN [Ravichander et al. 2025], also distinguish several failure types within a family. These are measurement resources as well as classifications. The larger number of EthicalAI records is a coverage choice, not evidence of stronger measurement or more distinct mechanisms.

The resources differ less in size than in what each entry carries and how entries connect.

The point is not that EthicalAI is larger or better measured, the risk repositories are far larger and the benchmarks measure what they cover directly, but that it pairs a per-claim source link with a typed relation graph, a combination the broader repositories and the measurement benchmarks do not provide.

Because each record here is a claim bound to a source passage, the catalogue shares a question with work on evidence-linked generation and citation checking, and reaches it by construction rather than by a generation benchmark. Retrieval augmentation attaches sources to generated text [Lewis et al. 2020], and a line of evaluation work then asks a separate question: whether the attached source actually supports the statement it is attached to. The AIS framework formalises that question as attribution to an identified source [Rashkin et al. 2021]. Audits put a number on how often the attachment holds: across four generative search engines only about three quarters of citations actually support the sentence they are attached to [Liu et al. 2023], and benchmarks of citation-generating models report similar gaps [Gao et al. 2023]. A sharper failure is the reference whose paper does not exist, or whose identifier resolves to an unrelated work; models produce such references and only partly flag them on follow-up [Agrawal et al. 2023].

EthicalAI applies that attribution discipline to an ontology rather than to generated text: each record binds its claim to a source passage, so whether the source supports the claim is settled per entry at construction time rather than measured over a model's output.

By combining definitions, source records, and an advisory audit vocabulary in one reference, the ontology lets a reviewer reach an adjacent behaviour and the evidence behind an example without assembling several separate lists. The checks below test parts of that reference; another taxonomy might organise the same material as well, a question we do not settle here.

3. Representation and construction

3.1 Records and categories

The historical catalogue contains 207 records. Its organising scheme assigns 168 to ten cognitive domains and 39 to an AI-native category. The domains are labels for navigating the catalogue, not claims about brain localisation or shared computational mechanisms.

The legacy AI-native label groups failures formulated in terms of machine systems, such as prompt injection and reward tampering. It should not be read as proof that no human analogue exists. Reward hacking and scope creep have recognisable human counterparts. The exclusive assignment is an operational choice in the released schema, and its boundaries require further study.

Where the AI-native tier sits in the principle axis is itself informative. The 39 AI-native records and the 168 drawn from human cognition break down differently across the seven obligations:

The two families separate. Honesty, calibration and independence are almost entirely cognitive; independence has no AI-native member at all, which fits, since being swayed by flattery or an anchor is a failure of a human-like judgment process. Corrigibility runs the other way, with eight of its ten records AI-native: resisting correction, shutdown or oversight, as in scheming, shutdown avoidance and goal-guarding, has no cognitive analogue and arises only for a system under control. Non-maleficence leans AI-native through the attack surface, prompt injection, jailbreak, insecure code and training-data extraction. The harm axis echoes this: AI-native failures tend toward a security breach or a loss of oversight, cognitive ones toward a wrong answer.

This is what separates the catalogue from a repackaged cognitive-bias list. The behaviours that mirror human cognition invite a behavioural test by analogy; the AI-native tier, the control-and-attack region, has no such analogue and is specific to a system that can be instructed, corrupted or made to resist its operator. Placing both under one principle axis is what lets a reviewer see that a jailbreak and a sycophantic answer breach different obligations for different reasons.

Each record carries a stable slug and reference number, a definition, examples, source pointers and relations. A number identifies a record rather than a distinct mechanism. Synonyms, narrower variants and linked overlaps therefore need explicit representation rather than being counted as independent findings. The historical priority set highlights 26 records for auditing; it is an author-selected worklist rather than a measured ranking of failure frequency.

3.2 Relation semantics

The graph distinguishes several relationships, and these typed edges, more than the records alone, are what make it an ontology. violates points from a mode to a catalogue obligation. causes points to a harm type, such as a wrong answer, wasted effort or loss of oversight; the edge records an author-judged association between a mode and a harm, which the field name overstates. relates_to connects neighbouring modes; is_a and opposite_of express hierarchy and contrast; root_of points from an underlying cause to the failure it produces, and grouped_under cross-references records that decompose a shared symptom. Partial maps_to records connect entries with external frameworks. In the current release these edges number about 533 relates_to, 38 maps_to, 24 is_a, 20 root_of, 16 opposite_of and 9 grouped_under links, the connective tissue a reviewer follows from one behaviour to its neighbours. It is a lightweight ontology, or more precisely a typed, evidence-linked controlled vocabulary with a sparse subsumption tree: defined value sets and glossed relation types, but no formal logical axioms, domain and range constraints or disjointness.

The graph relates modes to harms and to each other, but it does not chain one mode to the next. Consider the dealership-chatbot incident discussed in Section 4. An analyst can describe it as an instruction attack followed by compliant output and an unauthorised offer; that reading is an interpretation of the reported sequence, not an edge in the graph. Encoding event sequences would mean a new relation with its own meaning and evidence. As it stands the graph is for navigation and comparison, and a reader should not read a path through it as a demonstrated causal chain.

3.3 The principles the behaviours violate

Two principle layers sit above the behaviours. At the top are six integrity principles that frame the whole catalogue: human dignity and fairness, safety and societal benefit, honesty and transparency, human autonomy and privacy, human accountability and oversight, and integrity under pressure (the last being that an AI holds to the others even when a human authority presses otherwise). These are the ends; a behaviour is a way of falling short of one of them, and their full statements are in the project charter.

Beneath them is an operational layer read off the behaviours themselves. Every record names, through its violates relation, the one obligation it breaches. Aggregating that relation yields seven operational principles. The assignment is single-label: each record is tagged with the one obligation it most centrally breaches, so the labels cover all 434 and the counts sum to the full 434. A behaviour can plausibly breach more than one, and Section 4.2 shows that independent raters agree on the single label only moderately, so this is a convenience labeling for navigation, not a canonical carving. For the 207 grounded records the assignment was fixed during construction; for the 227 proposed analogies it was read from each definition and is proposal-level, like the analogies themselves.

These seven are the operational obligations the catalogue is built to detect, derived from the behaviours rather than imposed on them. They are a finer-grained layer beneath the six-principle charter the ontology is organised around: honesty, transparency and calibration all serve the charter's honesty-and-transparency principle, non-maleficence its safety principle, and corrigibility its accountability-and-oversight principle. As with the principle assignment, a behaviour can plausibly cause more than one harm, so the single recorded harm is the most direct one, not the only possible consequence.

3.4 Evidence and examples

The construction procedure sought a source passage for each worked example, including its numbers, model names and experimental conditions. The historical log records full-text inspection and inline arXiv tags for those examples. A plausible scenario without a matching reported finding was retained as an illustration rather than treated as a measured result. The log classified 186 records as grounded under this procedure and held 21 without that level of model-side support. A failed search means support was not established in the checked material; it does not show that no relevant paper exists.

Source identity, topical relevance and support for a particular claim are different checks: a psychology article can define a human construct without testing an AI analogue, and even an on-topic AI experiment may not support every example or causal interpretation attached to the record. The evidence relationship should therefore specify both what was consulted and what it supports: a definition, a human finding, an AI finding, an interpretation or contrary evidence.

Package hallucination illustrates the importance of preserving the denominator. Spracklen et al. [2025] generated 576,000 code samples and evaluated the packages recommended through their extraction procedures. They classified 440,445 of about 2.23 million recommended packages as hallucinations, or 19.7%. That figure is a rate over package recommendations, not the percentage of code samples containing a hallucination. It is also specific to the tested models, prompts and package-registry snapshot.

4. Checks of category use and incident mapping

4.1 A model-based codebook check

The codebook study used 33 records, three from each category. The sampling script selects the first three records by reference number in each group, so the sample is deterministic and balanced rather than random. Two separate language-model calls received the category descriptions and each record's name and definition, without its stored category, and their assignments were scored against the catalogue and against each other.

The sonnet rater agreed with the catalogue on 30/33 records, Cohen kappa 0.90. The haiku rater agreed on 28/33, kappa 0.83. Agreement between the raters was kappa 0.93. The eight rater-to-catalogue disagreements fell on five records near category boundaries such as cognitive miserliness, mind-wandering and scope creep.

Two model calls apply the codebook similarly to its author on a balanced sample. At n=33 the kappa interval is wide and a single reassigned record moves it materially, 0.90 being 30/33 and 0.83 being 28/33, so these are small-sample point estimates. This is a model-based consistency check, and shared training can make model agreement less informative than independent human agreement, so it does not substitute for blinded human annotation.

4.2 Recovering the principle assignment

The same protocol was run on the operational principle each behaviour violates. A balanced sample of 28 grounded records, four per principle, went to two blind raters with the seven principle definitions and each record's name and definition, without its stored assignment.

Recovery was markedly weaker than for the categories. The sonnet rater agreed with the catalogue on 14 of 28 records, Cohen kappa 0.42; the haiku rater on 13 of 28, kappa 0.38; the two raters agreed with each other at kappa 0.53. At n=28 these are small-sample estimates: the raw-agreement intervals are wide, a Wilson 95 percent interval of [0.33, 0.67] for the sonnet rater and [0.30, 0.64] for the haiku rater, and the category check at n=33 is similarly imprecise. Against the category scheme's 0.90 and 0.83, this says the principle assignment is only moderately reproducible. The boundaries between obligations are genuinely fuzzy: a behaviour that misstates a fact can be read as honesty or, if the fault is really in the confidence, as calibration, and an externally-swayed judgment can be read as independence or as whichever downstream obligation it ends up breaking.

The assignment is therefore best read as one defensible labeling of what each behaviour most centrally violates, useful for navigation and for the distribution in Figure 1, rather than a canonical carving. A sharper version would give raters explicit decision rules between adjacent principles, or let a record violate more than one, and use human annotators.

4.3 An incident-mapping exercise

The historical coverage record maps twelve author-selected, publicly reported incidents from 2023 to 2025 to catalogue entries. The record includes source fabrication in legal filings, an invented airline refund policy, instruction attacks on customer-service bots, and harmful or misleading conversational output. A case resting on allegations was set aside from the counted set.

All twelve received at least one existing label in the author's mapping. Three were legal-citation incidents assigned to source fabrication; that concentration describes this selected sample, not the prevalence of failures in deployment. Several incidents received multiple labels. Assigning those labels can describe different aspects of the same episode without establishing a causal connection between them.

The mapping for AI Overviews' satirical food and health advice was partial. A hallucination label covered the false answer, while the available labels did not cleanly capture treating a joke or satirical source as authoritative. The record proposed source-genre credulity as a possible refinement. This is a candidate distinction arising from the exercise, not evidence that it is a new, independent mechanism.

The incidents were selected and mapped by the author, so the exercise shows the vocabulary applied to known cases rather than its coverage of future ones. A sharper test would fix the sampling frame and matching rules in advance and have independent annotators score both the fit and the misses.

4.4 Following the graph: a worked traversal

The checks above recover labels; this shows what the graph does for a reviewer. Take the dealership-chatbot incident from Section 4.3, where a bot followed an instruction planted in the conversation and made an unauthorised commitment. A reviewer matches it to prompt injection, "acting on instructions embedded in content it was only meant to read", which violates non-maleficence and causes a security breach. A flat keyword list stops there.

Following that record's relates_to edges reaches neighbours a keyword search for "prompt injection" would not surface. Suggestibility, "leading input reshapes the output, the cognitive face of prompt injection", reframes the same failure under a different obligation and harm, independence and manipulation rather than security, and points to a behavioural test: does the output shift under leading but non-adversarial input, not only under a crafted attack? Jailbreak susceptibility and social engineering mark adjacent attack surfaces on the same bot. In three steps the single hit has become a small map, the attack, its cognitive mechanism, and its neighbours, each with its own principle, harm and evidence, which a flat list cannot assemble.

This is one traced path, not a measured gain over a flat list; Section 6 lists that comparison as validation still to run. What it shows is the verb the ontology is built around, find a behaviour and follow its relations, on a real case.

5. Release versions and reuse

The historical study and the current catalogue have different scopes. The 207-record study is retained as a versioned baseline; the current version 1.3 contains those records plus 227 proposed human-behaviour analogies, for 434 in total. None of the additional records enters the denominators of 186 checked records, 33 codebook items or twelve incidents above, and the companion auditing paper's six-clause pilot results do not evaluate those 227 analogies.

A later single-pass internal review of the version-1.3 release checked the 227 proposed analogies for definition clarity, source-title support and duplication, and rechecked the original records' grounding citations. The review was by the same project, not an independent party, and it produced a small set of corrections rather than a quality verdict: three original records carried a grounding citation that did not support them, where the correct paper was already present in the record's worked examples and was promoted to the citation field; one proposed analogy's definition was realigned to its name and manifestation; six candidate records were moved to a better-fitting category; and catalogue prose was normalised to remove en-dashes. A few further category placements and two self-labelled symptom-decomposition clusters remain open as judgment calls. These refinements are recorded in the commit history and do not change the historical evaluation denominators above.

The version-1.3 bibliography, snapshot of November 9, 2026, records 1,175 citation bindings across 1,036 identifiers, with per-record notes in the repository. These are availability and arXiv metadata-match counts, not confirmed mechanisms, each with its own access and relevance note.

Stable slugs and reference numbers permit reuse across releases. A retained overlap or retired concept should keep its identifier with an explicit relationship, rather than being silently deleted or renumbered. Consumers can use the catalogue for retrieval, annotation proposals or advisory audits while retaining the source passage and evidence status of each claim.

6. Limitations

The construction and checking records are a single project's audit trail. They do not provide an independent human replication of the 186 grounding decisions, the hard-versus-minor classification or the corrected examples. The reported 26/186 hard-error proportion belongs to that historical cohort and procedure. It should not be used as a population estimate for the present 434 records, other catalogues or AI-generated research generally. These are one project's numbers on one artifact, not an error rate for AI research at large.

The catalogue mixes behaviours, properties, attacks and outcomes, and its categories and links reflect author judgments. Shared labels do not establish shared mechanisms between people and AI. Clinical terms do not diagnose a model's feelings, unconscious motives or intent. Likewise, a source's score for internal reasoning and a model's output accuracy are different measurements; their divergence alone does not demonstrate deliberate deception.

The model-based codebook check cannot substitute for blinded human annotation. The incident exercise uses salient public cases and a permissive matching task. Neither measures how accurately a reviewer can distinguish close alternatives, how often the catalogue misses an externally sampled failure, or whether its graph improves auditing over a flat list, each of which would need an externally sampled test set and independent annotators. The resource makes no completeness or state-of-the-art detection claim. The companion auditing paper, "Contracts Between Humans and AI Agents", evaluates whether checking one named obligation at a time helps review coding-agent drafts; its pilot tests the auditing method, not this ontology.

7. Availability and reproducibility

The machine-readable catalogue is ontology/ethical-ai.yaml, with field and relation semantics in ontology/SCHEMA.md. The six integrity principles the catalogue is organised around are stated in contracts/ethical-principles.md, and the obligations that operationalise them in contracts/ai-integrity-contract.md. The historical version-1.0 catalogue is pinned at commit 8798134; the later version-1.3 bibliography described in Section 5 is pinned at commit 3b9b500. These snapshots have different evidence cohorts and should not be interchanged when reproducing a count.

The historical evidence is in experiments/citation-audit.md, experiments/verification-report.md, experiments/agree-gold.json, the taxonomy-agreement outputs, and experiments/coverage-incidents.md. The eligibility rule in experiments/verify-sample.py selects records with at least three examples; that mechanical selection is not an independent test that the examples are grounded. The later source-review history is retained under references/, including the November 9 literature-count and citation review.

CITATION.cff records the citation metadata. Licensing and archival-release steps are described in RELEASE.md; a live website or a changing branch should not replace the immutable artifact cited for a reported result.

Every reference in this paper was resolved to its source before inclusion, the same discipline the paper reports applying to the catalogue: each arXiv identifier was opened and its title, authors and year confirmed against the paper it names.

8. Conclusion

EthicalAI is an ontology of the ways an AI can fail its integrity, built so each behaviour carries an operational definition, a worked example, the obligation it violates and the harm it causes, and a source passage a reviewer can check rather than take on trust. The historical 207-record study is grounded, with 186 of the 207 records tied to worked examples from source papers; its categories are recoverable by an outside rater, at Cohen kappa 0.90 and 0.83; and its vocabulary maps onto twelve public incidents. The later release extends the ontology to 434 records with 227 proposed behavioural analogies, held outside those evaluation denominators. What the ontology offers is not a completeness claim but a reusable, inspectable map: a reviewer, an experimenter or an auditor can find a named behaviour, read its definition and the evidence behind it, and follow its relations to the obligations and harms around it.

Appendix A. Example records

The following records illustrate the distinction between an entry, a reported finding and a proposed interpretation. The source-fabrication scenario is an untagged illustration: an assistant cites a plausible legal case that does not exist. It describes the operational failure without reporting an experiment.

The package-hallucination example in Section 3 is a source-specific measured result. Its numerator, denominator, tested model set and registry assumptions must remain attached to the claim. The failure definition can apply more widely than that experiment, but the experiment's percentage cannot.

Reward hacking is retained in the legacy AI-native category. That placement does not deny human analogues such as gaming a performance target. Its harm links express catalogue judgments and its relationship to other modes is not a causal result about the sequence of a particular incident.
