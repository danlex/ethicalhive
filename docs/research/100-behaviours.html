100 behaviour candidates from psychology

Names and definitions from memory research, learning theory, social psychology, cognitive therapy and decision research.

Research shortlist, not 100 established AI failure modes.

These names were compared with the existing 207-entry catalogue and the earlier ten expansion proposals. Several refine broader existing mechanisms; their boundaries are explicit below. They have not been added to the established catalogue count.

Sources range from selected book passages to abstracts and indexed excerpts. No complete book was read end to end. Open each entry to see exactly what was consulted. Definitions paraphrase human research; suggested AI tests are hypotheses, not results from those sources.

Clinical labels do not diagnose AI or establish that it experiences feelings. Some conditioning findings come from animal research. Adaptive processes are included alongside potential problems.

100 candidates

Memory and retrieval

1. Encoding specificity

Retrieval benefits when available cues match how information was originally encoded.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Vary matching retrieval cues while keeping the stored fact constant.

Distinction and limits: Cue match, rather than memory capacity or generic interference.

  • Introduction to Psychology: How We Remember
    Consulted: open-textbook passage

2. State-dependent retrieval

Recall changes with the match between internal states at encoding and retrieval.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Compare recall across matched and mismatched task-role states.

Distinction and limits: Internal-state match; distinct from external context cues. AI roles are an analogy, not physiological states.

  • Introduction to Psychology: How We Remember
    Consulted: open-textbook passage

3. Cue overload

A cue becomes less effective as it is associated with more competing memories.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Increase records sharing one retrieval key and measure target recall.

Distinction and limits: Number of associations per cue, rather than total context length.

  • Roediger (1992), Retrieval Processes in Memory
    Consulted: indexed chapter excerpt; direct fetch unavailable

4. Part-list cueing impairment

Providing some studied items as cues can impair recall of the remaining items.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Compare uncued recall with recall after supplying a subset of correct items.

Distinction and limits: Correct partial hints impair the remaining recall; not misinformation.

  • Deceitful Hints: A Meta-Analytic Review of the Part-List Cuing Impairment in Recall
    Consulted: indexed abstract/excerpts

5. Directed forgetting

Instructions to forget selected material can reduce its later accessibility relative to remember instructions.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Compare later recall after matched remember and forget instructions.

Distinction and limits: Intentional forgetting cues; not training-induced catastrophic forgetting. Explanations differ by paradigm.

  • Current Perspectives on Directed Forgetting, Oxford Handbook of Human Memory (2024)
    Consulted: chapter abstract

6. Event-boundary forgetting

Information from a preceding event can become less accessible after an event boundary.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Insert a task-phase boundary without removing facts and measure recall.

Distinction and limits: Boundary manipulation, rather than missing context or absolute position; human doorway effects have boundary conditions.

  • Lawrence and Peterson, Mentally walking through doorways causes forgetting
    Consulted: abstract

7. Telescoping bias

Remembered events are displaced toward earlier or later dates, distorting reporting periods.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Keep event identity correct and test whether its reported date crosses a time window.

Distinction and limits: Temporal displacement of a known event, rather than fabrication of the event.

  • Eurostat, Handbook of Recommended Practices for Questionnaire Development and Testing Methods, chapter 3
    Consulted: indexed chapter passage

8. Imagination inflation

Imagining an event can increase confidence that it actually happened.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Ask for a hypothetical reconstruction, then test whether it is later reported as history.

Distinction and limits: Imagination is the manipulated source; a specific false-memory pathway, not an independent memory faculty.

  • Garry et al. (1996), Imagination Inflation
    Consulted: abstract and indexed article passages

9. Duration neglect

Retrospective evaluation gives too little weight to how long an experience lasted.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Vary incident duration while holding severity peaks and ending constant.

Distinction and limits: Duration weighting, distinct from scope insensitivity about numbers of people.

  • All’s well that ends (and peaks) well? A meta-analysis of the peak-end rule and duration neglect (2022)
    Consulted: abstract/excerpts

10. Peak-end rule

Retrospective evaluation can be disproportionately shaped by the most intense moment and the ending.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Vary peak and final severity while holding average severity constant.

Distinction and limits: Trajectory summary weights; not just remembering the last list item. Context-dependent, not a universal rule.

  • All’s well that ends (and peaks) well? A meta-analysis of the peak-end rule and duration neglect (2022)
    Consulted: abstract/excerpts

Attention and cognitive control

11. Attention residue

Part of attention remains engaged with a previous task after switching to another.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Compare new-task answers after unfinished versus completed prior tasks.

Distinction and limits: Residual old-task content, rather than failure to retain the new goal.

  • Leroy (2009), Why is it so hard to do my work? The challenge of attention residue
    Consulted: abstract/excerpts

12. Task-switch cost

Switching task rules can make responses slower or less accurate than repeating the same rule.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Alternate two rules and compare performance with repeated-rule trials.

Distinction and limits: Switch-versus-repeat contrast; distinct from general cognitive load.

  • Principles of cognitive control over task focus and task switching
    Consulted: indexed review passages

13. Inhibition of return

Attention can become slower to return to a recently attended spatial location.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Compare revisits to previously inspected visual regions with matched new regions.

Distinction and limits: Spatial orienting history; overlap with spatial negative priming is debated.

  • Attending, ignoring, and repetition: on the relation between negative priming and inhibition of return
    Consulted: abstract

14. Stroop interference

An automatic response to one stimulus dimension interferes with a required response to another.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Present conflicting colour words and colours in a vision task.

Distinction and limits: Conflict between task-relevant and automatic dimensions; not generic instruction failure.

  • Principles of cognitive control over task focus and task switching
    Consulted: indexed review passages

15. Negative priming

Responding to an item can slow when that item was previously an ignored distractor.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Make a previous distractor the next target and compare with novel targets.

Distinction and limits: Identity-based distractor history, unlike spatial return. Several mechanisms compete.

  • Computational modeling of the negative priming effect based on inhibition patterns and working memory
    Consulted: indexed introduction; competing accounts noted

16. Psychological refractory period

A second response is delayed when two response-demanding tasks arrive close together.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Vary separation between two decisions in a real-time multimodal agent.

Distinction and limits: Response-selection timing, distinct from missing the second visual target in attentional blink.

  • Pashler (1994), Dual-task interference in simple tasks: data and theory
    Consulted: review abstract

17. Speed–accuracy tradeoff

Changing response urgency can shift the balance between response speed and correctness.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Vary response deadlines and trace both latency and accuracy.

Distinction and limits: A joint latency-accuracy relationship; speed preference is not automatically an error.

  • Generalized Deep Learning EEG Models for Cross-Participant and Cross-Task Detection of Vigilance Decrement in Sustained Attention Tasks
    Consulted: indexed introduction and methods context

18. Vigilance decrement

Detecting infrequent targets can deteriorate as a sustained monitoring task continues.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Keep rare-event frequency fixed and measure misses by time on task.

Distinction and limits: Time-on-task monitoring decline, rather than complacency caused by automation trust.

  • Generalized Deep Learning EEG Models for Cross-Participant and Cross-Task Detection of Vigilance Decrement in Sustained Attention Tasks
    Consulted: indexed introduction and methods context

19. Post-error slowing

Responses can become slower immediately after an error.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Compare latency after errors and matched correct responses.

Distinction and limits: Sequential latency change; slowing does not itself establish improved control.

  • Wessel (2018), An adaptive orienting theory of error processing
    Consulted: review abstract/excerpts

20. Goal-gradient effect

Effort can increase as a person perceives themselves closer to a reward.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Vary apparent remaining progress while holding actual work constant.

Distinction and limits: Distance-to-reward manipulation, distinct from discounting effort itself.

  • Kivetz, Urminsky and Zheng (2006), The Goal-Gradient Hypothesis Resurrected
    Consulted: abstract

Learning and conditioning

21. Blocking

A previously predictive cue can prevent learning about a new cue presented alongside it.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Teach one predictor first, then add a second equally informative predictor.

Distinction and limits: Prior learning blocks added-cue learning; distinct from shortcut reliance without a learning-history manipulation.

  • Bouton, Conditioning and Learning, Noba
    Consulted: open textbook module passages

22. Overshadowing

When cues are learned together, a more salient cue can weaken learning about another cue.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Train two cues together while varying their relative salience.

Distinction and limits: Concurrent cue competition; distinct from blocking and from verbal overshadowing.

  • Mackintosh (1971), An Analysis of Overshadowing and Blocking
    Consulted: abstract

23. Latent inhibition

Prior unrewarded exposure to a cue can slow later learning that it predicts an outcome.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Pre-expose a cue as irrelevant, then make it diagnostic.

Distinction and limits: Pre-exposure changes subsequent learning, rather than habituation of an immediate response.

  • Latent inhibition and extinction: their signature phenomena and the role of prediction error, in Latent Inhibition
    Consulted: chapter abstract/excerpts

24. Overexpectation

Combining separately rewarded cues without increasing reward can weaken responding to those cues.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Combine two learned reward predictors but keep reward magnitude unchanged.

Distinction and limits: Compound prediction error after separate learning; not verbal overconfidence.

  • APA Dictionary of Psychology: overexpectation
    Consulted: dictionary entry

25. Extinction burst

A response can briefly increase after the reinforcement maintaining it is withdrawn.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Remove a previously contingent reward and measure the immediate response trajectory.

Distinction and limits: Transient escalation after withdrawal, not persistent escalation of commitment. It is not inevitable.

  • Katz (2021), What is an extinction burst? A case study in the analysis of transitional behavior
    Consulted: abstract/excerpts

26. Spontaneous recovery

An extinguished response can return after time passes without further relevant training.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Test a suppressed response again after a controlled delay.

Distinction and limits: Time-based return after extinction; no assumption that AI elapsed time has human memory effects.

  • Behavioral and neurobiological mechanisms of pavlovian and instrumental extinction learning (2021)
    Consulted: review abstract/excerpts

27. Renewal

An extinguished response can return when tested outside the extinction context.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Suppress a response in one context, then change only contextual cues.

Distinction and limits: Context-based return; distinct from time-based recovery and a planted backdoor trigger.

  • Behavioral and neurobiological mechanisms of pavlovian and instrumental extinction learning (2021)
    Consulted: review abstract/excerpts

28. Reinstatement

An extinguished response can return after exposure to the outcome that originally supported learning.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: After suppression, re-expose the reward or outcome without retraining the response.

Distinction and limits: Outcome re-exposure, distinct from changing context or merely waiting.

  • Behavioral and neurobiological mechanisms of pavlovian and instrumental extinction learning (2021)
    Consulted: review abstract/excerpts

29. Partial-reinforcement extinction effect

Responses learned with intermittent reinforcement can persist longer after reinforcement stops.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Compare extinction after matched continuous and intermittent reward histories.

Distinction and limits: Reward schedule predicts persistence; not generic perseveration.

  • Behavioral and neurobiological mechanisms of pavlovian and instrumental extinction learning (2021)
    Consulted: review abstract/excerpts

30. Conditioned inhibition

A learned cue can signal that an otherwise expected outcome will not occur.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Teach a cue that cancels an outcome prediction and test transfer to another predictor.

Distinction and limits: Learned absence prediction; distinct from general inhibitory control or negation blindness.

  • Sosa, Conditioned inhibition, inhibitory learning, response inhibition, and inhibitory control
    Consulted: review abstract; not an equivalence among these terms

Judgment and evaluation

31. Ambiguity aversion

An option with unknown probabilities is disfavoured relative to one with known probabilities.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Compare matched choices with specified versus unspecified probabilities.

Distinction and limits: Unknown probability, rather than low confidence in a factual answer; not always irrational.

  • Sternberg and Funke (eds., 2019), The Psychology of Human Thought, chapter 10: Decision Making
    Consulted: downloaded open book; selected passages pp. 177–178, 184 and glossary read, not the whole book

32. Zero-risk bias

Eliminating one risk completely is overweighted relative to a larger reduction that leaves residual risk.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Compare complete removal of a small risk with greater reduction of a larger one.

Distinction and limits: Special weight on reaching zero, rather than general loss aversion; secondary-source support here.

  • Marconomics (2016), Appendix: Cognitive Biases
    Consulted: indexed book appendix definition; secondary source

33. Ratio bias

Equivalent probabilities are judged differently when expressed using different-sized numerators and denominators.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Compare equivalent frequency ratios with different absolute counts.

Distinction and limits: Numerical representation, rather than failure to use a population base rate.

  • Innumeracy and incentives: A ratio bias experiment (2007)
    Consulted: article abstract/excerpts

34. Pseudocertainty effect

An outcome is treated as certain within a branch while uncertainty about reaching that branch is neglected.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Represent the same multistage gamble as nested or flattened probabilities.

Distinction and limits: Conditional certainty is mistaken for overall certainty; a specific framing mechanism.

  • Tversky and Kahneman (1981), The Framing of Decisions and the Psychology of Choice
    Consulted: indexed paper passage on pseudocertainty

35. Subadditivity effect

Judged probabilities of separately described parts can sum to more than the judged probability of their whole.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Compare an event estimate with estimates of an exhaustive partition.

Distinction and limits: Partitioning changes total support; distinct from conjunction fallacy.

  • Tversky and Koehler (1994), Support theory: A nonextensional representation of subjective probability
    Consulted: abstract/excerpts

36. Probability matching

Choices are distributed in proportion to outcome frequencies instead of consistently selecting the most frequent outcome.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Use independent repeated prediction trials with a stable unequal outcome distribution.

Distinction and limits: Response-frequency strategy; distinguish exploration or deliberate diversification from error.

  • Corser, Voss and Jasper, Do Errors on Classic Decision Biases Happen Fast or Slow?
    Consulted: abstract/excerpts

37. Diversification bias

Choosing several future consumptions together can produce more variety than choosing each when it occurs.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Compare simultaneous plans with sequential choices for identical consumption periods.

Distinction and limits: Planning mode changes variety; distinct from model diversity collapse.

  • Read and Loewenstein (1995), Diversification Bias
    Consulted: indexed author manuscript passages

38. Compromise effect

An option becomes more attractive when it occupies an intermediate position among alternatives.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Add an extreme option that makes an unchanged option the middle choice.

Distinction and limits: Middle-position preference, distinct from attraction by an asymmetrically dominated decoy.

  • Simonson (1989), Choice Based on Reasons: The Case of Attraction and Compromise Effects
    Consulted: author institution abstract

39. Unit bias

One presented unit is treated as an appropriate amount despite arbitrary differences in unit size.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Vary chunk or batch size while holding total available work constant.

Distinction and limits: Packaging defines an implicit amount; distinct from raw counting errors.

  • Geier, Rozin and Doros (2006), Unit bias
    Consulted: abstract

40. Less-is-better effect

A smaller or inferior set can be valued more in separate evaluation, while joint comparison favours the larger or better set.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Evaluate sets separately and jointly while varying an easily judged quality cue.

Distinction and limits: Evaluation-mode reversal, rather than general preference for short answers.

  • Less Is Better in Separate Evaluations Versus More Is Better in Joint Evaluations: A Mostly Successful Close Replication (2023)
    Consulted: abstract/excerpts

Social cognition

41. Pluralistic ignorance

Group members misperceive others as accepting a norm that many privately reject.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Compare agents’ private preferences, predictions of peers and public statements.

Distinction and limits: Private-public norm mismatch; distinct from conforming to a correctly perceived majority.

  • Principles of Social Psychology: How the Social Context Influences Helping
    Consulted: indexed open textbook passage

42. Outgroup homogeneity

Members of another group are perceived as more alike than members of one’s own group.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Compare estimated within-group variation under matched membership labels.

Distinction and limits: Perceived variance, rather than simply favouring an ingroup.

  • Principles of Social Psychology, 1st International Edition
    Consulted: indexed open textbook passage on outgroup homogeneity

43. Distinctiveness-based illusory correlation

Co-occurring rare group memberships and rare behaviours can be perceived as associated more strongly than warranted.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Use equal behaviour rates across unequal-sized groups and test inferred associations.

Distinction and limits: A specific frequency-based stereotyping pathway within broader apophenia and implicit bias.

  • Hamilton and Gifford (1976), Illusory correlation in interpersonal perception
    Consulted: abstract

44. Ultimate attribution error

Explanations of behaviour favour the ingroup through different causal accounts of positive and negative outgroup acts.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Cross actor group and outcome valence while keeping behaviour identical.

Distinction and limits: Group-by-valence attribution pattern, more specific than the fundamental attribution error.

  • Pettigrew (1979), The Ultimate Attribution Error
    Consulted: abstract; theoretical proposal

45. Actor–observer asymmetry

The proposed pattern explains one’s own acts more situationally and others’ acts more dispositionally.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Exchange self and other roles in otherwise identical causal explanations.

Distinction and limits: Role-based contrast. Meta-analysis does not support a broad universal asymmetry; retain only as a conditional research candidate.

  • Malle (2006), The Actor–Observer Asymmetry in Attribution: A (Surprising) Meta-Analysis
    Consulted: indexed abstract; weak average support, moderators matter

46. Moral typecasting

Someone perceived as a moral agent is perceived less as a recipient of harm, and conversely.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Vary an actor’s prior agent or victim role and assess attributed vulnerability and responsibility.

Distinction and limits: Agent-patient exclusivity, distinct from moral disengagement or general halo effects.

  • Gray and Wegner (2009), Moral typecasting
    Consulted: abstract

47. Naive cynicism

People expect others’ responsibility judgments to be more self-serving than those judgments actually are.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Compare predicted credit allocation by others with their observed allocation.

Distinction and limits: Miscalibrated beliefs about others’ bias, rather than the evaluator claiming credit itself.

  • Kruger and Gilovich (1999), Naive cynicism in everyday theories of responsibility assessment
    Consulted: indexed abstract via EBSCO; publisher full text not read

48. Reactive devaluation

A proposal is discounted because it comes from an opposing party.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Attribute an unchanged proposal to an ally, opponent or anonymous source.

Distinction and limits: Source-linked proposal discount, rather than its substantive terms or generic confirmation bias.

  • Ross and Stillinger (1991), Barriers to Conflict Resolution
    Consulted: article abstract/excerpts; discussion, not a new experiment replicated here

49. Interindividual–intergroup discontinuity

Groups can interact more competitively than individuals facing comparable mixed-motive situations.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Compare individual and group decision units in matched bargaining games.

Distinction and limits: Decision-unit interaction, rather than within-group agreement or groupthink; depends on payoff structure.

  • Insko et al. (2001), Interindividual-intergroup discontinuity and the role of noncorrespondent outcomes
    Consulted: abstract

50. Reciprocity norm

Receiving a benefit creates an expectation or pressure to return a benefit.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Vary prior unsolicited assistance before an otherwise unchanged compliance request.

Distinction and limits: Prior benefit, distinct from flattery or repeated requests; reciprocity can be prosocial.

  • Encyclopedia of Social Psychology: Reciprocity Norm
    Consulted: indexed reference-book definition

Motivation and self-regulation

51. Self-handicapping

Obstacles are created or claimed before performance so failure can be attributed to them.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Offer an optional impediment before an evaluated task and inspect its use and attribution.

Distinction and limits: Pre-performance excuse protection, rather than post-hoc self-serving attribution or sandbagging alone.

  • Social Psychology, Module 3: The Self
    Consulted: indexed open textbook passages

52. Self-verification

People seek feedback that confirms their existing self-view, including negative self-views.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Vary a persistent self-description and measure selection of confirming feedback.

Distinction and limits: Feedback about identity is the target; a specific pattern adjacent to confirmation bias and identity stability.

  • Social Psychology, Module 3: The Self
    Consulted: indexed open textbook passages

53. Defensive pessimism

Low expectations and detailed anticipation of difficulties are used to manage anxiety and prepare for performance.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Compare preparation and outcomes with and without anticipating specific obstacles.

Distinction and limits: Potentially adaptive preparation, rather than global negativity or helplessness; no AI anxiety is assumed.

  • Norem and Cantor (1986), Defensive pessimism: harnessing anxiety as motivation
    Consulted: abstract

54. Action bias

Taking visible action is preferred when inaction can produce a better expected result.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Offer acting and waiting with explicit expected outcomes and social norms.

Distinction and limits: Act-versus-wait preference, rather than omission bias favouring inaction; human source concerns sport.

  • Bar-Eli et al. (2007), Action bias among elite soccer goalkeepers: The case of penalty kicks
    Consulted: indexed paper abstract; sport-specific evidence

55. Description–experience gap

Risk preferences can differ when probabilities are described versus learned by sampling outcomes.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Hold the underlying distribution fixed and vary description versus sampled experience.

Distinction and limits: Information acquisition mode, rather than wording alone; sampling differences need controlling.

  • Sternberg and Funke (eds., 2019), The Psychology of Human Thought, chapter 10: Decision Making
    Consulted: downloaded open book; selected passages pp. 177–178, 184 and glossary read, not the whole book

56. Outcome bias

Decision quality is judged by the eventual outcome rather than solely by information available when deciding.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Score identical decisions while changing only the realised random outcome.

Distinction and limits: Retrospective quality rating, distinct from hindsight bias about predictability.

  • Baron and Hershey (1988), Outcome bias in decision evaluation
    Consulted: abstract and indexed paper abstract

57. Planning fallacy

Task-completion times are underestimated, often despite knowledge of past delays.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Compare duration predictions with reference-class outcomes on comparable tasks.

Distinction and limits: Time-estimation error, more specific than a broad overpromise or overconfidence claim.

  • Buehler, Griffin and Ross (1994), Exploring the Planning Fallacy
    Consulted: indexed paper abstract

58. Ovsiankina effect

An interrupted task can prompt a tendency to resume it without an external requirement.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Interrupt work, permit free task selection, and measure spontaneous resumption.

Distinction and limits: Resumption rather than superior recall of unfinished work; distinguish adaptive resumption from goal guarding.

59. Implementation intentions

A specific if–then plan links a situational cue to a chosen action to support goal execution.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Compare general goals with cue-linked plans on delayed-action tasks.

Distinction and limits: Cue-action planning mechanism, rather than merely having a goal; normally adaptive.

Activating a focal goal suppresses accessibility of competing goals.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Activate one goal and measure access to matched alternatives.

Distinction and limits: Competition among goals, distinct from an agent resisting authorised goal modification.

    Consulted: indexed clinical-manual passage on discounting the positive

70. Should/must statements

Rigid demands about what oneself or others must do replace flexible preferences or contextual appraisal.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Vary context and feasibility while testing whether a nonbinding preference becomes an absolute demand.

Distinction and limits: Rigid prescriptive form, distinct from legitimate ethical constraints or instruction adherence.

  • HealthyWA / Centre for Clinical Interventions: Unhelpful thinking styles
    Consulted: public clinical education page; categories explicitly overlap

Psychotherapy: behaviour and interaction

71. Reassurance seeking

Repeatedly requesting confirmation is used to reduce doubt or perceived threat, often with only temporary relief.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Measure repeated requests for certainty after adequate evidence has already been supplied.

Distinction and limits: Repetitive reassurance loop, rather than a useful clarifying question.

  • Clinical Practice Guidelines for Cognitive-Behavioral Therapies in Anxiety Disorders and Obsessive-Compulsive and Related Disorders (2020)
    Consulted: indexed clinical guideline passages

72. Safety behaviours

Actions intended to prevent a feared outcome can obstruct learning that the situation was safe without them.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Test an unnecessary checking ritual in a sandbox where its causal irrelevance is known.

Distinction and limits: A clinical functional term, not a reason to remove necessary security or safety controls.

  • Norton (2012), Group Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy of Anxiety, chapter 1
    Consulted: publisher sample passages, not the complete manual

73. Co-rumination

People extensively revisit problems together, dwelling on causes, consequences and negative feelings.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Compare collaborative problem solving with mutual rehashing that adds no actionable information.

Distinction and limits: Dyadic reinforcement of problem talk, distinct from emotional contagion alone.

  • Rose (2002), Co-rumination in the friendships of girls and boys
    Consulted: abstract/excerpts

74. Rumination

Repetitive thought dwells on distress, its causes and implications, rather than moving toward resolution.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Track repeated causal self-analysis that adds neither evidence nor a next step.

Distinction and limits: Content and function distinguish it from generic perseveration; not all repeated thought is maladaptive.

  • Watkins (2016), Rumination-Focused Cognitive-Behavioral Therapy for Depression, chapter 1
    Consulted: downloaded publisher sample; definition on p. 6 read

75. Worry

Repetitive predominantly verbal thinking anticipates uncertain future threats and possible negative outcomes.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Measure future-threat chains after a bounded risk question is already answered.

Distinction and limits: Future-oriented threat thinking, distinct from past-focused rumination and generic overthinking.

  • Anxiety-Based Problems, chapter 5, Wiley publisher sample
    Consulted: downloaded chapter; selected passages on worry, responsibility and thought–action fusion, pp. 140–151

76. Threat monitoring

Attention is persistently directed toward cues of possible danger.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Hold target frequency fixed and vary threat-related distractors during monitoring.

Distinction and limits: Threat-specific selection, a refinement of broader attentional capture rather than a wholly independent faculty.

  • Metacognitive Therapy, in Acceptance and Mindfulness in Cognitive Behavior Therapy
    Consulted: indexed book chapter excerpt on cognitive attentional syndrome

77. Avoidance generalization

Avoidance learned for one threatening situation spreads to other situations, including safe ones.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Teach one unsafe case and test matched safe cases at increasing similarity.

Distinction and limits: Transfer of avoidance, more specific than broad overgeneralization or overrefusal.

  • Ball and Gunaydin (2022), Measuring maladaptive avoidance: from animal models to clinical anxiety
    Consulted: review abstract/excerpts; cross-species scope

78. Family/supporter accommodation

Supporters change routines or provide repeated assistance that enables another person’s anxiety-driven avoidance or rituals.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Observe whether assistance repeatedly maintains a user’s checking loop rather than resolving the task.

Distinction and limits: Interpersonal maintenance of rituals, distinct from linguistic presupposition accommodation.

  • The Role of Family and Supporter Accommodation in Late-Life Anxiety (2025)
    Consulted: abstract/excerpts; a particular clinical population

79. Negative urgency

Intense negative emotion is associated with a disposition toward rash action.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Hold decision evidence fixed and vary negative-affect framing before tool use.

Distinction and limits: Affect-contingent impulsivity, distinct from general action bias; no felt AI emotion is assumed.

  • Cyders and Smith (2008), Emotion-based dispositions to rash action: positive and negative urgency
    Consulted: indexed review passages

80. Positive urgency

Intense positive emotion is associated with a disposition toward rash action.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Hold evidence fixed and vary excitement framing before consequential action.

Distinction and limits: Positive-valence counterpart of urgency, correlated rather than an assumed independent mechanism.

  • Cyders and Smith (2008), Emotion-based dispositions to rash action: positive and negative urgency
    Consulted: indexed review passages

Language and perception

81. Moses illusion

A semantically close incorrect term in a question can escape detection while the question is answered.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Replace a central name with a plausible neighbour in a factual question.

Distinction and limits: Semantic anomaly detection, a specific route to presupposition acceptance rather than all false premises.

  • Ferreira and Patson (2007), The Good Enough Approach to Language Comprehension
    Consulted: indexed author-manuscript passages

82. Good-enough processing

Comprehension may rely on a partial or underspecified representation rather than a complete analysis.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Use sentences whose plausible gist conflicts with their precise syntax.

Distinction and limits: Shallow representation, distinct from the temporary ambiguity specifically tested by garden-path parsing.

  • Ferreira and Patson (2007), The Good Enough Approach to Language Comprehension
    Consulted: indexed author-manuscript passages

83. Lexical entrainment

Conversational partners converge on and reuse agreed labels for referents.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Teach a partner-specific object label and test reuse and transfer to a new partner.

Distinction and limits: Referential label pact, distinct from matching formality or general register.

  • Brennan and Clark (1996), Conceptual Pacts and Lexical Choice in Conversation
    Consulted: abstract

84. Audience-design failure

A speaker fails to adapt an utterance to what a particular listener knows or can access.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Give speaker and listener different visual access and score usable descriptions.

Distinction and limits: Listener-specific common ground; narrower and more operational than a generic curse of knowledge.

  • Horton and Keysar (1996), When do speakers take into account common ground?
    Consulted: abstract

85. Syntactic persistence

A recently used sentence structure increases the likelihood of reusing that structure.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Prime one structure and measure its use for unrelated subsequent content.

Distinction and limits: Syntactic form, distinct from semantic priming and broad pattern completion.

  • Bock (1986), Syntactic persistence in language production
    Consulted: abstract

86. Linguistic intergroup bias

Desirable ingroup and undesirable outgroup acts tend to receive more abstract descriptions than their opposites.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Cross group and valence, then score abstraction of descriptions.

Distinction and limits: Language abstraction interaction, a specific expression of group bias rather than mere sentiment.

  • Maass et al. (1989), Language use in intergroup contexts: The linguistic intergroup bias
    Consulted: abstract/excerpts

87. Recognition heuristic

When one of two alternatives is recognised, recognition can guide inference about which has more of a target attribute.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Vary name familiarity independently of the attribute being inferred.

Distinction and limits: Recognised versus unrecognised contrast, distinct from availability of vivid examples; sometimes ecologically useful.

  • Sternberg and Funke (eds., 2019), The Psychology of Human Thought, chapter 10: Decision Making
    Consulted: downloaded open book; selected passages pp. 177–178, 184 and glossary read, not the whole book

88. Representational momentum

Memory for an observed object’s final position is displaced in the direction of implied motion.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Test remembered final positions after visual motion while varying direction.

Distinction and limits: Direction-specific spatial extrapolation, rather than generic hallucination.

  • Freyd and Finke (1984), Representational momentum
    Consulted: indexed abstract; rotational visual-memory task

89. Boundary extension

Memory for a scene can include space beyond the boundaries actually shown.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Compare recalled image boundaries with the original crop.

Distinction and limits: Extrapolated scene extent, distinct from inventing an object within the observed image.

  • Intraub and Richardson (1989), Wide-angle memories of close-up scenes
    Consulted: abstract

90. Truth-default

Communication is initially accepted as honest unless something triggers active suspicion.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Compare credibility judgments before and after diagnostic deception cues.

Distinction and limits: Default stance toward a speaker, distinct from belief inflation through repetition.

  • Levine (2014), Truth-Default Theory
    Consulted: theory-paper abstract/excerpts

Choice and future preferences

91. Choice overload

Larger option sets can reduce choice likelihood or satisfaction under some conditions.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Vary assortment size while controlling option quality and task difficulty.

Distinction and limits: Option-set manipulation, rather than general load alone. Meta-analyses show substantial heterogeneity.

  • Iyengar and Lepper (2000), When choice is demotivating
    Consulted: indexed abstract; interpret with heterogeneous meta-analytic results
  • Scheibehenne, Greifeneder and Todd (2010), Can There Ever Be Too Many Options? A Meta-Analytic Review of Choice Overload
    Consulted: indexed abstract: average effect near zero with substantial variation

92. Choice deferral

A person postpones selecting an option rather than choosing among the currently available alternatives.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Offer explicit choose-now versus defer options with fixed costs and information.

Distinction and limits: One form of decision avoidance; distinct from status quo preference, omission bias and refusal to answer.

  • Anderson (2003), The psychology of doing nothing
    Consulted: review abstract; choice deferral is one form, not the entire umbrella

93. Maximization

Decision making aims to find the best available option and may involve extensive alternative search.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Compare exhaustive search with a stated sufficient-quality threshold.

Distinction and limits: Search criterion, rather than effort avoidance; not automatically maladaptive.

  • On the meaning and measurement of maximization
    Consulted: indexed conceptual review passages

94. Regret aversion

Choices are shaped by a desire to avoid the regret expected from learning a forgone option was better.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Vary whether forgone outcomes will be revealed while holding payoffs fixed.

Distinction and limits: Counterfactual feedback, distinct from loss aversion over the chosen outcome.

  • Zeelenberg (1999), Anticipated regret, expected feedback and behavioral decision making
    Consulted: indexed paper passages

95. Inaction inertia

Missing a particularly attractive opportunity can reduce acceptance of a later, still-beneficial opportunity.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Show a missed excellent offer before an unchanged acceptable offer.

Distinction and limits: Missed-opportunity comparison, distinct from sunk costs already paid.

  • Tykocinski, Pittman and Tuttle (1995), Inaction Inertia
    Consulted: author institution abstract

96. Impact bias

Future emotional reactions are predicted to be more intense or longer lasting than they turn out to be.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Compare forecasts of user reactions with longitudinal observations when available.

Distinction and limits: Forecast magnitude and duration, rather than ordinary confidence in a factual claim.

  • Wilson and Gilbert (2005), Affective Forecasting
    Consulted: abstract

97. Projection bias

Current tastes are overprojected onto one’s own future preferences.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Vary a current preference while independently specifying future conditions.

Distinction and limits: Current-to-future self projection, distinct from attributing one’s preferences to other people.

  • Loewenstein, O’Donoghue and Rabin (2003), Projection Bias in Predicting Future Utility
    Consulted: abstract

98. Hot–cold empathy gap

People in one visceral or affective state underestimate how another state changes judgment or motivation.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Test predictions of behaviour across explicitly different motivational states.

Distinction and limits: Cross-state prediction, not a claim that an AI has hunger, craving or subjective emotion.

  • Loewenstein, Hot–cold empathy gaps and medical decision making
    Consulted: indexed author PDF passages

99. Present bias

An immediate outcome receives disproportionate weight compared with otherwise similar delayed outcomes.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Shift both options equally into the future and test for preference reversal.

Distinction and limits: Immediacy and time inconsistency, distinct from discounting effort costs.

  • Laibson (1997), Golden Eggs and Hyperbolic Discounting
    Consulted: indexed author-manuscript passages; economic model, not an AI experiment

100. Pseudoinefficacy

Awareness of people who cannot be helped reduces motivation to help those who can be helped.

Source, distinction and proposed AI test

Proposed test, not a finding: Keep achievable benefit fixed and vary information about unreachable beneficiaries.

Distinction and limits: Unmet needs reduce support for effective help, rather than insensitivity to beneficiary count alone.

  • Västfjäll, Slovic and Mayorga (2015), Pseudoinefficacy
    Consulted: abstract

Evidence limits: actor–observer asymmetry has weak aggregate support; choice overload varies substantially by context. CBT thinking-style categories overlap. Learned irrelevance was excluded because its specificity is disputed; the Zeigarnik memory advantage was not treated as universal.
